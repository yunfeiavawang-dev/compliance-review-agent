## 1. Purpose

The Financial Action Task Force (FATF) Recommendations require countries and financial institutions to adopt a risk-based approach (RBA) to AML/CFT. This document summarizes key FATF guidance relevant to Pacific Rim Financial Group's onboarding and risk assessment processes.

FATF Recommendations referenced: R.1 (Risk Assessment), R.10 (CDD), R.12 (PEPs), R.19 (Higher-Risk Countries), R.20 (STR Reporting).

## 2. FATF High-Risk and Grey List Jurisdictions

### 2.1 High-Risk Jurisdictions Subject to a Call for Action (Black List)

As of the latest FATF update, the following jurisdictions are subject to a call for counter-measures:
- Democratic People's Republic of Korea (DPRK)
- Iran
- Myanmar

For customers connected to these jurisdictions, the bank must apply counter-measures including: refusal of business relationship, enhanced scrutiny of all transactions, and mandatory reporting.

### 2.2 Jurisdictions Under Increased Monitoring (Grey List)

Jurisdictions under increased FATF monitoring include (subject to periodic updates):
- Bulgaria, Burkina Faso, Cameroon, Croatia, Democratic Republic of Congo
- Haiti, Kenya, Mali, Monaco, Mozambique, Namibia, Nigeria
- Philippines, Senegal, South Africa, South Sudan, Syria, Tanzania
- Turkey, Venezuela, Vietnam, Yemen

For customers connected to grey-list jurisdictions, enhanced due diligence is required but a blanket refusal is not mandated. The bank must assess whether the specific ML/TF risks are adequately mitigated.

### 2.3 Risk Assessment for Jurisdictions

Factors to consider beyond FATF listing:
- Level of corruption (Transparency International CPI score)
- Effectiveness of AML/CFT supervision and enforcement
- Prevalence of financial crime typologies (trade-based ML, cash-intensive economy)
- Existence of effective beneficial ownership registries
- Tax transparency and information exchange agreements

## 3. Risk-Based CDD Requirements

### 3.1 Principle of Proportionality

The extent of CDD measures should be proportionate to the assessed ML/TF risk:
- **Low risk**: Simplified CDD permitted — reduced verification, less frequent reviews
- **Standard risk**: Full CDD as prescribed by national law
- **High risk**: Enhanced CDD — additional documents, senior approval, deeper investigation, more frequent monitoring

### 3.2 When to Refuse or Terminate

A financial institution must refuse to establish (or must terminate) a business relationship when it:
- Cannot satisfactorily complete CDD — unable to verify the customer's identity or the identity of the beneficial owner
- Suspects that the funds or transactions are related to ML/TF
- Believes the customer is intentionally providing false or misleading information

In such cases, the institution should consider filing an STR.

### 3.3 Non-Face-to-Face Relationships

Where the customer is not physically present for identification, the bank must apply enhanced measures:
- Additional documents or information to verify identity
- Supplementary measures to verify or certify documents (e.g., certified copies)
- Requiring the first payment to be through an account in the customer's name at a regulated bank
- Independent electronic verification of identity

## 4. Trade-Based Money Laundering (TBML)

### 4.1 Typologies

FATF identifies the following TBML typologies as high risk:
- Over- or under-invoicing of goods and services
- Multiple invoicing for the same goods
- Falsely described goods and services
- Short-lived trading companies with disproportionate transaction volumes
- Use of free trade zones to obscure origin and destination of goods
- Round-tripping of funds through multiple jurisdictions

### 4.2 Red Flags for TBML

- Transaction values inconsistent with the size or nature of the business
- Payments to or from parties unrelated to the declared trade
- Reluctance to provide supporting trade documentation (invoices, bills of lading)
- Shipping routes inconsistent with the declared origin/destination of goods
- Goods transshipped through free trade zones without clear commercial rationale
- Sudden change in trading patterns or partners

## 5. Beneficial Ownership Transparency

### 5.1 FATF Recommendation 24 and 25

Countries should ensure adequate, accurate, and timely information on beneficial ownership of legal persons (R.24) and legal arrangements such as trusts (R.25).

Financial institutions must identify and verify beneficial owners by:
- Using company registry information as a starting point (but not sole reliance)
- Requesting constitutional documents, shareholder registers, and trust deeds
- Identifying the natural person(s) who ultimately own or control the entity
- Investigating nominee structures and bearer shares

### 5.2 Complex Structures

Multi-layered corporate structures require particular scrutiny:
- Chains of holding companies across multiple jurisdictions
- Nominee directors or shareholders
- Use of trusts or foundations with opaque governance
- Bearer shares or bearer instruments (high risk — many jurisdictions have banned these)
- Shell companies with no genuine business activity

The bank should trace the chain of ownership to the ultimate beneficial owner(s). If the chain cannot be established, the bank must consider whether to proceed with the relationship.

## 6. Proliferation Financing

### 6.1 Targeted Financial Sanctions for Proliferation

Banks must implement measures to comply with UN Security Council resolutions on proliferation financing, including:
- Screening against UNSC designations lists (DPRK, Iran)
- Freezing assets of designated persons and entities without delay
- Monitoring for dual-use goods and technologies
- Reporting to relevant authorities

### 6.2 Red Flags

- Transactions involving goods that could be dual-use (electronics, metals, chemicals, precision instruments)
- Shipping routes through or near DPRK, Iran, or Syria
- Front companies with minimal operational history
- Payment structures designed to obscure the end-user of goods
